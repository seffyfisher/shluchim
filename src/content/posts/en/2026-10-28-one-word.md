You told your shaliach to "clean up the table."
It did.
All of it.

One word can do that.
And the name of this blog is one of those words,
because in Hebrew slang, "shluch" is an insult.

(Shluchim, singular shaliach, means "emissaries" in Hebrew. It's what I call my AI agents.)

## Where it comes from:

I came across an article by Chen Malul on the National Library of Israel blog,
["Who are you calling 'Shluchim'?"](https://blog.nli.org.il/tashelhit/) (in Hebrew).

It opens like this:
"Many of us use the slur 'shluch' to describe a scruffy, neglected person."
(Chen Malul, National Library of Israel blog, my translation)

Same letters as my shluchim, read a little differently.

And there's a real community behind the word.
The Shluchim speak Tashelhit, a Berber language.
They live in southwestern Morocco, spread across about a thousand villages.

![Two clay heads, an older man in a cream turban and a woman in a colorful headscarf, smiling at each other over bowls of spices and dates at a market, on a mint background.](/images/posts/one-word-1-souk-1672.webp)

The Jews of the region were mostly traders who moved from village to village,
and they picked up Tashelhit and spoke it.

Take Chen Malul's grandmother, Ladicia.
She learned Tashelhit so she could talk with her mother-in-law.

## How a name becomes an insult:

According to the article, the French built roads that connected the villages to the cities.
People started moving to the city,
and when city people met villagers, "the shameful label was born."
(again from Chen Malul's article, my translation)

![A clay mountain village with a winding road down to the city, a donkey carrying baskets, and a folded old newspaper in front, on a sage green background.](/images/posts/one-word-2-road-to-city-1672.webp)

Immigrants from Morocco brought the nickname to Israel,
and it hurt Muslims and village Jews alike.

So the name of a whole community, with its own language and life together,
turned into a word for "neglected."

![A clay head of an older woman in a blue and yellow festive headdress with silver coin jewelry, next to a small clay teapot and two glasses of mint tea, on a lavender background.](/images/posts/one-word-3-festive-woman-1672.webp)

I really recommend the full article. It's worth your time.

## Death and life are in the power of the tongue:

That's a line from Proverbs, and Hebrew speakers use it all the time.

I want to be clear about one thing:
I'm not comparing people to my shluchim.

What got me is how much power one word has.
One word changed what a whole community's name means.

In a much smaller way, I see the same thing every day with my shluchim.

## Why this matters for shluchim:

A shaliach does what you wrote.
Not what you meant.

Tell it to "clean up the table,"
and maybe you get a tidy table with no duplicates.
Or maybe an empty one.

To the shaliach, both count as "cleaning up."
The difference is one word you didn't write.

## Examples from my team:

1. The Chef, the shaliach that does my grocery shopping, is always told to stop before paying.
Three words, and that's the difference between a cart that's ready and an order that went out before I saw it.

2. The invite to my group said you could ask there "where you got stuck."
A few words, and a group for conversation turned into a help desk.
I asked for it to come out, and now it's a place to talk and swap ideas.

3. My shluchim that can reach my messaging app have a two-word rule: "read only."
Without it, "take a look at the group" could end with a message a shaliach sent in my name.

4. When I tell a shaliach "log it in the warehouse," it knows exactly where that goes.
That's because we agreed up front what "warehouse" means, and it's been the same word ever since.

## OK, so how do you write an instruction that won't wipe your table:

1. Use a precise verb.
"Delete the duplicate rows," not "clean up."
"Move to archive," not "sort it out."

2. Spell out what's off limits too.
"Don't delete anything" or "read only" will save you a lot of headaches.

3. Stop before anything you can't undo.
Deleting, paying, sending in your name.
"Show me before you do it."

4. Ask it to explain in its own words what it's about to do.
If the explanation doesn't match what you meant, you caught it in time.

5. One word for each thing.
If you agreed on "warehouse," don't write "archive" tomorrow and "folder" the day after.

Words aren't a technical detail.
Not with people, and not with shluchim.

As for the name of this blog,
I'm glad I finally know the story behind it. 🙂
