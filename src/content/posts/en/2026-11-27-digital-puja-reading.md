Nir, or more accurately [Dr. Nir](https://iyyun.co.il/paper/%D7%A2%D7%9C-%D7%94%D7%9B%D7%95%D7%97-%D7%94%D7%99%D7%A6%D7%99%D7%A8%D7%AA%D7%99-%D7%A9%D7%9C-%D7%94%D7%AA%D7%A9%D7%95%D7%A7%D7%94-%D7%91%D7%9E%D7%97%D7%A9%D7%91%D7%94-%D7%94%D7%94%D7%95%D7%93%D7%99/), a friend of mine and a scholar of religions, told me about "digital puja."
It grabbed me right away.

![A lavender-gray shaliach head looking at a glowing book on a smartphone, beside a lit oil lamp, incense smoke, and orange petals, as notification icons fade away in the background](/images/posts/2026-11-27-digital-puja-reading-main-1672.webp)

Puja (even though the name sounds like a veggie omelet to me) is a Hindu ritual, and some people practice it online too.
He told me about a Harvard article describing how some practitioners prepare for the digital ritual: cleaning the computer and keyboard, closing programs, clearing browser history, cache, and cookies.
The really strict ones even restart the computer.

To them, a computer used for work and browsing (and I assume a few other things) needs to be purified before worship.
Clearing cookies suddenly takes on a religious meaning.
This isn't a rule for all Hindus, it's just what some practitioners do.
[The Harvard source](https://rpl.hds.harvard.edu/religion-context/case-studies/technology/internet-pujas).

I work on digital books and the reading app at <a href="https://www.e-vrit.co.il/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">e-vrit</a> (an Israeli Hebrew ebook service).
It's what I do day after day, for over a decade now, and this sent my mind straight there.
What is my experience when I open a book on a device where a second ago I answered a work message, or went back and forth with some rogue shaliach?

(Shluchim, singular shaliach, is Hebrew for "emissaries". It's what I call my AI agents.)

## Bell, flowers, click:

I went to ShreeGanesh to see how an online puja actually works.
An illustrated temple, burgundy background, gold frame, and Ganesha in the center.
On the side there's a bell, flowers, tilak, incense, and a lamp.
I clicked the flowers, the incense, and the lamp.
Flowers fell across the screen, smoke rose, and the lamp moved.
According to the site, there's sound too. [The interface and instructions](https://shreeganesh.com/site/ganesha-online-prayer/).

![Online puja interface on ShreeGanesh showing Ganesha and five ritual actions](/images/posts/2026-11-27-digital-puja-reading-shreeganesh-idle-1666.webp)

*ShreeGanesh, screenshot from November 27, 2026. Five actions next to the figure of Ganesha.*

The interface is simple, 2D.
Nothing fancy.
There are a few things to do, and you can take your time.
Click, wait, do another action.

![ShreeGanesh puja interface after activating flowers, incense, and the lamp](/images/posts/2026-11-27-digital-puja-reading-shreeganesh-active-1666.webp)

*The same interface after activating the flowers, incense, and lamp. Screenshot from my test.*

And who checks that I actually cleared my cookies?
I walked right in and offered everything without passing a purity check.
The study describes preparation a person does on their own, not software that enforces it.
The site I tested is a current example, and not necessarily the exact site studied back then.

It got me thinking a lot about microcopy (the small bits of interface text), and how even instructions that aren't enforced digitally still do something to us.
There's something deeper here, especially when it touches belief and inner motivation.

You can provide all the UI (user interface) in the world, and still leave the preparation and intention to the person, maybe just nudging their intent.

## The book on the nightstand:

In an app, on the surface, everything is straightforward.
I buy a book, download it, pick a font size, and read.
Except there are a few more things around it.

A book next to the bed waits there even when it's closed.
You can see how much is left.
There's a bookmark, a folded corner, the stack of pages still ahead.

On a phone, the book sits right next to work, messages, and the news.
You can reach it from anywhere.
You can also leave it in a second.
What it doesn't have is that clear physical presence and sense of space.

I don't think a phone should recreate everything a printed book has.
On the contrary, a page-flip animation doesn't fix it.
I want it to be easy to step into a book, stay in it, and come back tomorrow without losing my place, diving straight back in.

## Same reading, different place:

Reading before bed and reading on the go can happen in the same app, in the same book, even on the same page.
Around you, completely different things are happening.

By the bed, reading can be the transition from the day into sleep.
On the go, it can fill ten free minutes.
Sometimes it's quiet, sometimes it's noisy.
Sometimes you re-read the same sentence three times, or even more.

It's a very intimate act.
Someone wrote words, and I give them room inside my head.
My pace.
My associations.
Nobody on the outside sees what's actually happening in there.

Those are the things I want to remember when I work on a reading app.
What happens when I come back to a book?
When I stop in the middle?
How much crap did I put up with today just so I can simply keep reading?

That's what stayed with me from the puja story.
They moved the ritual to a screen, and people still found a way to prepare for it there, creating a religious experience even in a digital version.

Reading also has that moment of entry, a kind of regular ritual.
By the bed, on the bus, at my daughter's after-school class.
In my view, the ideal experience makes room for the mental ritual too: reading a book and disappearing completely.

I remember we literally added a tiny bit of loading time when opening a book instead of an instant pop-open, just to give you a moment.
In a second you're crossing into another world.
Take a breath, do your ritual, and come disappear with us into the pages.
