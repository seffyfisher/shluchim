It used to be that to smear someone without signing, you needed a bucket of glue and a wall.
Today all it takes is a journalist who doesn't exist.

(Shluchim, singular shaliach, is Hebrew for "emissaries". It's what I call my AI agents.)


## Where the word even comes from:

Pashkevil (the wall posters that cover ultra-Orthodox neighborhoods in Israel) sounds like a word born in an old Jewish town,
somewhere between the shtetl and the beit midrash (the Torah study hall).

That's what people assume in the Haredi (ultra-Orthodox) community, and most people just don't know otherwise.
I didn't know either.

So it turns out the root is in Rome.
In 1501, Cardinal Oliviero Carafa set up a battered ancient statue on the corner of a piazza,
and the Romans called it Pasquino.

People started sticking anonymous mocking verses on that statue, against the Pope and the Church.
They were called pasquinate (pasquinades), and from there the word made its way across Europe,
most likely through German (Pasquill), into Yiddish, and from there to us.

And the Greek part?
The word itself isn't Greek, it's the statue's nickname.
But the statue is a copy of a Hellenistic statue (from the Greek world after Alexander the Great),
and it probably shows Menelaus holding the body of Patroclus, a scene from the Iliad.

Meaning, every pashkevil in Mea Shearim (an ultra-Orthodox neighborhood in Jerusalem) stands, indirectly, on the shoulders of a Greek hero.

## How I found out:

AI revealed all of this to me in a moment.
For me, it was eye-opening.

## The 2026 pashkevil:

A good pashkevil works because it's anonymous.
You see the message, and you don't see who hung it.

On October 8, 2026, OpenAI published a report on two influence operations they blocked,
one from Iran and one from Russia.

An influence operation is an organized attempt to change public opinion,
without the public knowing who's behind it.

The Iranian operation ran seven "journalists" who don't exist.
They pitched long articles to small and medium news sites around the world,
and OpenAI found almost 100 articles published under their names, on about a dozen sites.

One of those sites had almost 2 million followers on Facebook.
Meaning, the pashkevil moved from the wall next to the synagogue to a news site with a real audience.

![A half-torn Hebrew pashkevil: 'Grave warning! Beware of journalists who do not exist. The article was written, the writer was never created.' Signed: the Source Checkers' Committee.](/images/posts/pashkevil-2026-02-fake-journalists-1024.webp)

The Russian operation went further.
It ran a "research institute" in Latin America, through a fake persona,
and the staff on the ground, according to the report, didn't know they were working for a Russian group.

They also produced fake "leaked" documents and scripts for recordings,
and some of the fakes spread far enough to trigger fact checks and official denials.

And now the line from the report that, in my view, is the whole story:
these operations looked a lot like influence operations from before the AI era,
and AI only made some of the work easier.

Same old trick.
Except today the pashkevil has a name and a bio.
The journalist just doesn't exist.

## The experiment: digital pashkevils:

The National Library of Israel has a [huge collection of scanned pashkevils](https://www.nli.org.il/en/discover/posters/pashkevils), 30,483 items in the catalog.
That collection inspired me.

I asked Codex to make digital pashkevils in that style,
with an image model (AI that makes images from text).
All of them, naturally, against AI.

The first rounds didn't work for me.
Pashkevils on a wall, pashkevils with characters of the shluchim. Not that.

I asked for half-torn paper only,
and sharper, funnier texts, in the style of the pashkevils from the archive.

The fourth round passed on the first try.
The Hebrew was checked word by word.
